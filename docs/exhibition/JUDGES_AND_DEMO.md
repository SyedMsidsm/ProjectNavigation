# RAIN exhibition field guide

## What the team built

RAIN stands for Risk-Aware Indoor Navigation. A student records a walk through a building, names places on the way and saves the map on an Android phone. Later, a visitor chooses a known starting point and a destination. The app estimates progress from footfalls and turns, follows the saved connections, gives directions and lets the visitor confirm their position if the estimate becomes uncertain. A first-time visitor to an exhibition, department office or laboratory is the user. A campus coordinator who knows the building is a plausible map maintainer. Campus deployment is a proposal; the demonstration runs on one phone.

Kotlin and Jetpack Compose provide the app and UI. Android sensors feed a distance and relative-heading estimator. The local map stores a graph of room marks, corners and corridor segments. A route planner operates on that graph. The destination assistant interprets words against the saved place catalog. OCR can read an existing physical sign. Android speech input and text-to-speech handle different jobs. Guided navigation follows route progress; the blue arrow represents relative phone direction after alignment. The schematic depicts recorded trails and door sides. Its lines do not establish room dimensions, wall positions or surveyed coordinates.

## Opening pitch (about 45 seconds)

“Suppose your exhibition invitation says ‘Research Centre, third floor.’ You reach the water filter and there are classrooms in both directions. What you need is one instruction: which way do I walk, and where do I turn? With RAIN, someone who knows the floor walks it once and marks the places visitors actually ask for. Now I can select the water filter and the Research Centre, confirm the destination, and see the first turn. If I pass it, RAIN helps me retrace my steps. We can also compare the shortest route with a route that may be easier to follow. Pick a destination on this floor and we’ll show you what the phone knows, and where it asks for your help.”

**Ten-second version.** “RAIN lets a campus guide record a corridor once and lets a visitor follow it later, using a phone, familiar room names and existing signs.”

**Close after the demo.** “Our next pilot would answer a practical question for a campus: do first-time visitors reach their destination with fewer wrong turns? We would measure that against ordinary directions before recommending a wider rollout.”

## Live walkthrough

1. Operator opens Saved maps and chooses College third floor. One presenter speaks, one operates, one watches the physical route and counts steps, and one handles the backup and questions. Agree who takes each role in rehearsal.
2. At the actual water filter, select Water filter → Research Centre → Shortest. Face down the first highlighted corridor before starting. Point out the remaining route distance versus distance to the next turn. If presenting from the stall, describe this as a preview and use a starting place you physically recognize before walking.
3. Walk with the judges if time and corridor conditions permit. Point out one room mark, the turning cue and the changing progress. An independently counted step total is useful evidence if someone asks about error. Never deliberately walk backward while watching the screen; turn or side-step safely and look where you are going.
4. For a missed-turn rehearsal in a clear corridor, pass the corner, acknowledge “I’m heading back,” walk back and confirm the corner when you recognize it. If you cannot identify it, choose a known place or scan a sign. At an endpoint, confirm physical arrival. A waveform from a moving hand can count as activity, so demonstrate holding the phone normally.
5. With the judges back at the stall, show recording: start a separate trail, mark left and right rooms, read a sign, review its name, and save a named endpoint. Keep the exhibition map available. Show “I want to pee” only if appropriate to the audience; “I need the washroom” demonstrates the same user need. “Where can I borrow a book?” demonstrates the model on a map containing a library.
6. Use the controlled two-path floor to explain risk trade-offs. On the exhibition map both planners can offer the same path. Have the backup on the presentation laptop, test playback offline, and rehearse the transition: “Here is the recorded app workflow; this portion uses demonstration inputs.”

## Questions judges may ask

| Question | Answer you can give |
|---|---|
| Why would anyone use this instead of asking a person? | A visitor can reuse the same directions, in their own time, even after the volunteer leaves. Staff still matter for first-time mapping, map updates and helping visitors who get lost. |
| What happens on day one in a building? | A coordinator must record or verify a usable map and mark recognizable places. A visitor can then choose among those places. There is no automatic understanding of an unfamiliar building. |
| How do you know where I am initially? | You select a known place or review a matching sign. The phone then estimates movement relative to that anchor. An incorrect start can invalidate the route until you correct it. |
| What exactly is one step? | Android can supply step-detector events. The app also has a software detector for peaks and troughs in acceleration and checks cadence. Three consistent startup peaks can be credited together. This reduces isolated sway and explains the delayed count after a pause. |
| How are metres calculated? Why could two walks on the same floor differ? | Counted footfalls times the stride setting gives an estimated distance. Stride varies with the person, turns and pace. A missed or extra peak also changes distance. Calibration helps with average stride, not every individual error. |
| Does turning the phone count as turning my body? | They can differ. We use gravity-referenced gyro rotation, walking evidence and alignment with the first corridor. A hand-held phone facing along the walk is the supported exhibition posture. Pockets, bags and phone repositioning remain field-test cases. |
| Why does the arrow move while I stand still? | It indicates the phone’s relative facing direction. A valid step changes position. Changing facing direction alone should not advance position. Re-align after changing your grip or interrupting guidance. |
| Can it know I walked backward without turning? | A step event has no forward/backward sign. After a detected overshoot the user explicitly chooses return mode. Subsequent accepted steps reduce the estimated distance back, even without a full phone rotation. Recognition at the landmark confirms the correction. Walking the wrong way after selecting return can still fool the estimate. |
| What if I miss a turn, overshoot a room or walk into an unknown corridor? | A mismatch between expected turns, distance and observed motion can flag a problem. A known overshoot offers a retrace estimate. For uncertain location use a known room or sign and replan. It cannot locate you anywhere in an unmapped corridor. |
| How does a hall become a junction? | A junction is a shared node with multiple walkable edges. A compatible recorded reversal that retraces a known segment can reuse that corridor. The recorder can also explicitly reconnect at a recognized junction. Similar-looking or spatially close rooms alone are insufficient evidence. |
| Why Dijkstra? Would A* be better? | There are nonnegative route costs and small floor graphs. Dijkstra gives the minimum under the selected cost. Including the incoming directed edge in the search state lets a turn depend on how the visitor reached the junction. A* could reduce search on larger maps with a suitable lower bound. |
| What does “risk” mean? Is this the safest route? | It means estimated wayfinding difficulty. The heuristic uses junction complexity, turn angle, sign readability, nearby similar doors and segment length. It does not assess crowds, fire, emergency exits, personal security or wheelchair suitability. |
| Can you explain the risk calculation on a whiteboard? | For one decision let L be walking length, p the heuristic score, λ its cost weight, c the cost of asking for a check, and e its expected effectiveness. Compare λp with c + λp(1−e). A recognizable sign can justify the cheaper check. Accumulate distance and the chosen penalties across the route. A missing sign gives no benefit from a scan. We have not calibrated p as a real probability. |
| If shortest and risk-aware look identical, is the feature fake? | On a tree there is only one simple path between two nodes. Both should choose it. On our controlled test floor the software selects 35 m versus 40 m, with lower estimated difficulty for the longer route. Whether that reduces human errors is a separate evaluation. |
| Won’t extra room marks distort risk? | They can change the graph segmentation and heuristic features. Compare equivalent floor maps with different marking density before treating scores as comparable between buildings. This is a limitation worth measuring. |
| What AI model is running and why a small one? | Gemma 3 1B instruction-tuned, quantized to 4 bits, runs locally using LiteRT-LM on the S24 CPU. Its job is narrow: map language to an existing place ID. A smaller model has a practical storage and compute footprint. The model file is provisioned separately from the APK. |
| Is every answer generated by Gemma? | Exact names, aliases, fuzzy matching and common restroom requests have deterministic handling. Other indirect language can go to Gemma. Model output is checked against saved IDs, and the visitor confirms the destination. A valid but semantically wrong suggestion is still possible. |
| If there are two washrooms or no washroom? | Matching saved facilities can be offered for selection. An absent destination requires another saved place. An unavailable facility should never turn into invented map geometry. |
| What happens with italic logos, fancy lettering, glare, or two signs together? | ML Kit reads visible text. Stylized lettering, blur, tiny text and glare can cause empty or wrong results. Increase readable size, change angle, improve lighting and retry. Review or edit the result; use manual selection if uncertain. A logo with no readable text needs a separate validated visual recognition system. |
| Could a sign saying “Room 301” identify the wrong floor? | Yes. Similar names make OCR ambiguous. Confirm the floor, landmark and candidate; a text match alone cannot establish physical position. Sparse or duplicated labels need a human choice. |
| What if the internet, camera, microphone or model is unavailable? | Core routing uses the stored graph in the app. Typed selection works without the microphone, manual marking without a camera, and a saved-place list without Gemma. Offline speech depends on installed device language resources. Verify the exact demo phone in airplane mode before claiming that configuration. |
| Is the map 3D? Can it see furniture or walls? | The phone renders a schematic map with perspective cues and zoom. Steps record walked connectivity and approximate lengths. Measuring room footprints requires extra inputs such as verified plans or surveyed geometry. There is no obstacle detection guarantee. |
| How does RAIN differ from GPS, beacons, QR codes or AR? | RAIN’s core workflow uses a previously recorded graph, phone motion and existing labels. Beacon or QR deployments can provide stronger anchors but need installed infrastructure. Camera-based AR can add visual motion evidence under suitable conditions; its field performance needs separate testing. |
| What if the building changes? | The map owner must update and reverify the affected paths. A closed door or moved sign can make a saved route unusable. The demo does not maintain a live facilities feed. |
| Are photos or private maps uploaded? | The core workflow keeps saved maps and the language model locally. The scanner processes frames on device. The debug build writes local diagnostic events for investigation. Operator-exported logs and recordings require care, especially around people and private rooms. |
| What evidence do you have? | Distinguish software tests, on-device UI checks, individual physical walks and controlled user studies. A 22/22 walk happened once, and other walks had errors. A passing route test proves a graph decision, not the user’s location. We have not established a campus-wide accuracy figure. |
| What experiment would convince you? | Label two routes with measured lengths and turns. Counterbalance shortest versus risk-aware order across unfamiliar users. Measure wrong turns, completion, time, prompts, arrival error and recovery success. Separately measure stationary false positives, footfall error and turn error by pace, device and carry mode. |
| Is this safe for blind users or emergency evacuation? | We have not validated either use. Voice cues can help users look at the screen less. Those higher-stakes settings require accessibility co-design, obstacle awareness, verified maps and specialist evaluation. |
| What is your contribution if Android, OCR and Gemma already exist? | Our contribution is the workflow connecting user-recorded walkable graphs, relative motion, validated destination choice, risk-weighted routing and landmark-based recovery. We can demonstrate which component made each decision. |
| Have you published it? | The team prepared a project technical paper. Cite publication or acceptance only if the team can produce the venue and confirmation. |

**When you don't know.** “We haven't measured that on this configuration. What we have verified is ___. The test that would answer your question is ___.” Use the actual map or test case if you can show it in under a minute.
